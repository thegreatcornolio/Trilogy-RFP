Executive Summary

We understand Represent to be one of the UK's fastest-growing fashion brands, having scaled from a screen-printing project in Greater Manchester in 2011 into a British luxury streetwear label generating close to £100m in revenue, up from £80.8m in 2023 and £48.4m the year before. The brand has flagship stores in Soho London, Manchester, and West Hollywood, wholesale partnerships with Selfridges, Harrods, and Harvey Nichols, and a loyal global community underpinned by its Prestige loyalty programme and over 300,000 members. With a minority investment from private equity firm True and ambitions to build toward a multi-billion-pound business, Represent is entering a phase where operational consistency matters as much as creative momentum.

What comes through clearly is that Represent's customers love the product and the brand's identity — the quality, the community, the “owners leading from the front” ethos. But the numbers tell a more mixed story on service: Represent's Trustpilot score of 3.8 out of 5 sits well below what we'd expect for a brand positioned at this level of luxury and loyalty. For a business built on repeat purchase and community, that gap between brand promise and delivered experience is a meaningful commercial risk, not just a reputational one.

Our research surfaced a consistent pattern behind that score. Customers report slow and sometimes unresponsive email and chat support, with some describing being ignored for days or weeks at a time. Returns and refunds are a particular pressure point — your own team publicly acknowledged a “huge backlog of returns” following a Vault sale event, and this pattern repeats around peak periods, most visibly over Christmas 2025, when a cluster of reviews described automated-only replies and no resolution at all. Courier and delivery issues also frequently escalate into customer service complaints when the follow-up process is slow. The common thread across all of these is not a lack of skill or care — many individual support and in-store staff are warmly praised by name — but a lack of capacity and consistency at exactly the moments, drops, sales, and holiday peaks, when Represent's business model creates the most demand.

We understand that Represent currently has 18 FTE supporting customers out of Mauritius, and that you are not entirely satisfied with the current arrangement. That tells us two useful things: first, that Represent has already validated the case for offshore support, so this is a conversation about improving outcomes, not convincing you of the model. Second, that the current setup may simply not have the depth or flexibility to absorb Represent's peak trading patterns.

This is where we believe the case for shifting to Cape Town is strongest. Mauritius has a population of only around 1.2 million and a BPO sector historically built around finance and back-office “follow-the-sun” processing rather than deep, always-on retail customer experience — which may explain why capacity buckles at exactly the moments Represent needs it most. South Africa's talent pool is an order of magnitude larger, giving real headroom to scale support well beyond 18 FTE as Represent grows toward its own £250m ambition, without hitting the ceiling Mauritius is structurally prone to. South Africa also sits at GMT+2, far closer to the UK than Mauritius's GMT+4, giving much better live overlap with UK shift patterns, UK management, and the evening and weekend peaks that matter most for a drop-driven fashion brand. On the commercial side, while South Africa typically runs somewhat higher in cost than smaller offshore markets, international buyers consistently choose it specifically for its multilingual capability and fluent accent alongside its favourable time zone — and as Represent deepens its footprint across UK and European markets, a South African team's fluency across English, Afrikaans, German, Dutch, and French offers considerably broader language coverage than Mauritius's English/French bilingual base.
